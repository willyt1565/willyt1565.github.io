How to Switch Property Managers in Texas Without Losing Your Tenant

Most owners who are unhappy with their property manager stay longer than they should. Not because they're satisfied, but because switching sounds risky: "What happens to my tenant? My deposit? This month's rent?" Here's the short answer. You can switch property managers in the middle of a lease in Texas. Your tenant's lease stays in force, and the new manager simply takes over. What protects you is the order you do things in: read the termination terms, line up the new company, give proper written notice and make sure the deposit, lease, keys and records transfer cleanly. This is how to do it step by step.

In short

  • Changing managers doesn't change the lease. The tenant, rent and end date stay the same.
  • Check your current agreement for the notice period, termination fees and any clause that keeps paying the old company for a tenant they placed.
  • The security deposit is your legal responsibility as the owner, even if the old manager is holding it. Get it transferred and documented.

Signs it's time to switch

Every manager has an off week. A pattern is different. If several of these sound familiar, it's time to look at your options:

  • You can't get a straight answer or a call back
  • Owner statements are late, confusing or hard to reconcile with your bank deposits
  • Repair bills seem high, or you suspect markups you were never told about
  • You haven't seen an inspection report or photos of your home in a year or more
  • Vacancies drag on longer than they should, with little explanation
  • Your tenant tells you their repair requests are being ignored

That last one matters more than owners realize. A tenant who feels ignored is a tenant who doesn't renew, and turnover is one of the most expensive events in the life of a rental.

Step 1: Read your management agreement

Before you call anyone or send anything, pull out your current management agreement and look for these clauses:

  • Termination notice period. Many agreements require 30 to 60 days' written notice. Some only allow termination at certain times, such as the end of a lease.
  • Termination fees. Some companies charge a flat fee, some charge a percentage of the fees they would have earned, and some charge nothing at all.
  • Tenant placement clauses. This is the one that surprises owners. Some agreements say that if the company placed your current tenant, they're owed a fee, or even their monthly percentage, for the rest of that lease or any renewal. Know this before you commit to a start date.
  • How notice must be delivered. Email, certified mail or a specific form. Notice sent the wrong way may not count.

If anything is unclear, have a Texas real estate attorney review the agreement. A short consultation costs far less than a fee dispute.

Step 2: Line up your new manager first

Don't terminate your current company until your new one is ready to start. Interview managers, compare the full yearly cost (not just the monthly percentage) and sign with the new company before you send notice. Then pick a start date that lines up with the end of your notice period, so there's no gap in rent collection, maintenance coverage or someone answering your tenant's calls.

Timing tip: Aim for the handover to land at the start of a month, after the current month's rent has been collected and paid out. It keeps the rent ledger clean and avoids a month where nobody is sure who collected what.

Step 3: Give written notice

Send notice exactly the way your agreement requires, and keep proof of delivery. Be brief and professional. You don't need to list your reasons or argue your case. State that you're terminating the agreement, the effective date, and that your new manager will contact them to coordinate the transfer of the lease, security deposit, keys and records.

Step 4: Make sure everything transfers

This is the step that matters most, and it's where switches go wrong. Your new manager should coordinate directly with the old one to collect:

  • The signed lease and all addenda, including the Texas flood disclosure
  • The tenant's application and screening file
  • The move-in condition report and photos
  • The security deposit. As the owner, you're ultimately responsible for returning your tenant's deposit under Texas law, even if your old manager is holding it. Texas requires the deposit to be refunded, or deductions itemized in writing, within 30 days after the tenant moves out (Tex. Prop. Code §92.103). Make sure the full deposit is transferred to your new manager and documented, so you don't end up refunding money you never received.
  • Any prepaid rent, including last month's rent if the tenant paid it upfront
  • The rent ledger and full payment history
  • Open work orders, appliance and home warranties, and vendor contacts
  • All keys, garage remotes, gate codes and mailbox keys
  • HOA contacts and any open violation notices

Ask for a final owner statement showing the closing balance of your account, and compare it with your own records before the old company releases funds.

Step 5: Tell your tenant

Your tenant needs to know three things: who to pay, how to request repairs and who to call in an emergency. A good new manager sends a welcome letter with all of it and sets the tenant up in the new resident portal before the first rent payment is due. Texas law also requires landlords to disclose the name and street address of the company managing the property when a tenant asks (Tex. Prop. Code §92.201), and putting it in writing upfront takes care of that.

In our experience, most tenants welcome the change, especially if repairs have been slow. A short, friendly introduction and a quick response to their first request go a long way toward a renewal.

Step 6: Get a baseline inspection

Once your new manager takes over, schedule a walk-through with photos. It gives you a clear record of the home's condition at the handover point, catches deferred maintenance the old company may have missed and protects you if there's a deposit dispute when the tenant eventually moves out.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Canceling before the new manager is ready. A gap in coverage means missed rent, unanswered repair calls and a confused tenant.
  • Not confirming where the security deposit is held. Get the amount and the transfer in writing.
  • Forgetting prepaid rent. If your tenant paid last month's rent upfront, that money needs to move too.
  • Leaving the tenant unsure where to pay. Payments sent to the old company after the handover are a headache for everyone.
  • Skipping the agreement review. A placement clause you didn't know about can mean paying two companies at once.

Switching managers is mostly paperwork and timing. Handled in the right order, your tenant barely notices anything changed, except that their calls get answered. Frequently asked questions

Can I switch property managers in the middle of a lease in Texas?

Yes. The lease is between you and your tenant, and the property manager is your agent. When you change managers, the lease, rent amount and end date stay exactly the same. Only the company collecting rent and handling repairs changes.

Will switching property managers affect my tenant's lease?

No. The lease terms stay in force. Your tenant keeps paying the same rent under the same lease; they just pay a different company and send repair requests through a new portal.

How long does it take to switch property management companies?

Usually as long as the notice period in your current management agreement, often 30 to 60 days. Once the old company cooperates, the actual handover of leases, deposits, keys and records can be done in a few days.

Who is responsible for the security deposit when I change property managers?

You are. As the owner, you are ultimately responsible for refunding your tenant's security deposit under Texas law, even if your former manager is holding it. Make sure the full deposit is transferred to your new manager and documented in writing.

What if my old property manager won't hand over records or the deposit?

Put your request in writing and keep copies. If they still don't cooperate, talk to a Texas real estate attorney. Property management companies in Texas operate under a broker licensed by the Texas Real Estate Commission (TREC), which accepts consumer complaints.
