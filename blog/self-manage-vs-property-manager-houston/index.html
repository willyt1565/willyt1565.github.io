Houston Owners

Should You Self-Manage Your Houston Rental or Hire a Property Manager?

By the Newera Property Management team · October 21, 2026 · 9 min read

If you own a rental in Houston, you've probably asked yourself this question: "Why would I give someone 8% of my rent when I can just do this myself?" It's a fair question. Plenty of owners self-manage successfully. But the fee is the only cost of management most people can see, and the expensive mistakes rarely show up until month eight or nine of a lease. Here's an honest look at what it really costs to run a Houston rental, and how to tell which path makes sense for you.

What does a property manager actually cost in Houston?

In the Houston market, full-service management usually runs somewhere between 8% and 10% of collected rent, with some companies charging less and adding fees elsewhere. On top of the monthly fee, most managers charge a leasing fee when they place a new resident (commonly around one month's rent in Houston, sometimes less for owners on full management) and a renewal fee when a lease is extended.

Our own management fee is 8% of monthly rent, with portfolio discounts down to as little as 6% for owners with multiple properties. Leasing and renewal fees are quoted during a free consultation, because they depend on the property. We don't mark up vendor invoices and we don't charge administration fees.

So on a home renting for $2,400 a month (close to the Houston average for a single-family rental), an 8% fee is $192 a month, or about $2,300 a year. That's the number most owners focus on. It's the right place to start, but it's only half of the math.

The question to ask any manager: "What will I pay in a full year, including leasing, renewal, inspections and maintenance coordination?" A low monthly percentage with a long list of add-on fees often costs more than a slightly higher, all-in rate.

What does self-managing really cost?

Self-managing isn't free. You're paying in time, in risk and, most of the time, in vacancy. These are the costs owners underestimate most.

1. Vacancy is the most expensive line item

According to HAR's July 2026 rental report, single-family rentals in the Houston area averaged 37 days on market, with more than 9,800 homes available for lease. Renters have choices right now. A home that's priced a little high, shows poorly online or has a slow response time on inquiries sits longer.

Here's the math that matters: one extra month of vacancy on a $2,400 home costs you $2,400. That's roughly a full year of management fees, gone in a single turnover. Pricing correctly on day one, getting quality photos up fast and answering leads the same day are where professional leasing pays for itself.

2. Screening mistakes cost more than vacancy

The resident you choose decides how the next 12 months go. An owner in a hurry to fill a vacancy is the owner most likely to skip the employment verification, take a story at face value or accept a partial deposit. If that lease goes bad, you're looking at lost rent, court costs, turnover repairs and another vacancy, often stacked on top of each other.

Texas also updated its eviction process. Senate Bill 38, effective January 1, 2026, changed how eviction cases move through justice court. Among other things, notices can only be delivered electronically if both sides agreed to it in writing, uncontested cases can be decided faster and appeals have stricter requirements. The details matter: a notice delivered the wrong way can push your case back to the start. Consistent, written screening criteria (applied the same way to every applicant, in line with fair housing law) is the best protection, because it prevents most evictions before they start.

3. Texas landlord rules have real penalties

Texas is considered a landlord-friendly state, but the Property Code is specific, and the penalties for getting it wrong are real. A few that catch Houston owners most often:

  • Security deposits. You have 30 days after the resident moves out to refund the deposit or send a written, itemized list of deductions, and you can't deduct for normal wear and tear. A landlord who acts in bad faith can owe $100, plus three times the amount wrongly withheld, plus the resident's attorney's fees (Tex. Prop. Code §92.103–92.109).
  • Flood disclosure. Since January 1, 2022, Texas landlords must give a separate written flood disclosure at or before lease signing. It must state whether you know the home is in a 100-year floodplain and whether you know of flooding at the home in the last five years. If you skip it and the resident suffers substantial flood damage to their belongings, they can terminate the lease (§92.0135). In Houston, this isn't a formality.
  • Repairs. When a resident who is current on rent reports a condition that affects health or safety, the law presumes seven days is a reasonable time to make a diligent effort to fix it (§92.056). In August, a broken AC is a health and safety issue, and residents know it.
  • Rekeying. Exterior locks must be rekeyed within seven days of each tenant turnover, at the owner's expense, and Texas requires specific security devices such as keyless deadbolts and door viewers (§92.153–92.156).

4. Houston adds its own layer

Owning a rental here, whether it's inside the Loop or out in Katy, Cypress, Spring, Pearland or Sugar Land, comes with a few things owners in other markets don't think about:

  • Hurricane and storm season. June through November, you need a plan for who checks the property after a storm, who documents damage for insurance and who talks to the resident. If you live out of town, this is where self-managing gets hard fast.
  • Heat and humidity. AC systems, roofs, gutters and foundations work harder here. Small leaks become mold problems quickly. Regular inspections catch the issues residents don't report.
  • Property taxes. If you're converting your former home into a rental, plan for a higher tax bill. A home rented to tenants on January 1 doesn't qualify for the homestead exemption for that year, which means losing the school district exemption (now $140,000) and the 10% annual cap on taxable value. Talk to the Harris County Appraisal District (or your county's district) and your tax professional before you move out.

5. Your time has a price

Showings on weekday evenings, a water heater call on Sunday morning, chasing late rent, scheduling vendors, keeping records for your CPA. In a quiet month it might be a few hours. During a turnover it can be full days. If you're self-managing one home near where you live, that may be fine. If you have a demanding job, more than one property or you live outside Houston, it adds up.

When does self-managing make sense?

We'll be straight with you. Self-managing can be the right call if:

  • You live close to the property and can respond in person within a day
  • You have reliable, licensed vendors you already trust
  • You're comfortable with Texas lease, deposit and fair housing rules, and you keep good records
  • You have a long-term resident who pays on time and takes care of the home
  • You actually enjoy the work

If most of those describe you, you may not need full management. Some owners in that position use a tenant placement (leasing-only) service instead: a professional handles marketing, screening and the lease, then hands the home back to you.

When is a property manager worth it?

Management usually pays for itself when one or more of these is true:

  • You live out of town, out of state or are relocating
  • You own more than one rental, or plan to
  • Your home has been vacant longer than expected, or your last resident didn't work out
  • You don't have time for evening showings and weekend repair calls
  • You want documented inspections, clean monthly statements and someone who knows Texas rules handling the paperwork

What to ask before you hire a Houston property manager

If you decide to interview managers, these questions separate the good ones quickly:

  1. What's your total cost for a year, including leasing, renewal and any other fees?
  2. Do you mark up maintenance or vendor invoices?
  3. How often do you inspect the home, and do I get photos?
  4. Who answers an emergency call at 2 a.m., a person or a voicemail?
  5. How long does it take you to fill a vacancy, and how do you set the rent?
  6. When do I get paid each month, and what does my statement show?
  7. Are you a licensed Texas broker? (You can verify any license on the TREC website.)

For reference, here's how we answer them: an 8% management fee with leasing and renewal fees quoted upfront, no vendor markups, inspections every six months documented with photos in your owner portal, a 24/7 emergency line answered by a real person, and owner payouts typically by the 10th of the month once rent has cleared. All services are provided under a licensed Texas broker (TREC #9016567-BB).

The bottom line

The 8% is easy to see. Vacancy, a bad placement, a deposit dispute or a missed flood disclosure are harder to see, and any one of them can cost more than a year of management. Run the numbers on your own property, be honest about your time and how close you live, and choose the option that protects the asset. If you'd like a second opinion on your rent price or your numbers, we're happy to walk through it with you, whether or not you end up hiring us.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a property manager cost in Houston?

Full-service property management in Houston typically runs about 8% to 10% of collected rent, plus a leasing fee when a new resident is placed (commonly around one month's rent) and a renewal fee when a lease is extended. Newera charges 8% of monthly rent, with portfolio discounts down to 6%, and quotes leasing and renewal fees during a free consultation.

Is 8% a good property management fee in Houston?

8% is in line with the Houston market. The better comparison is the total yearly cost, including leasing, renewal, inspection and maintenance markups. A low percentage with many add-on fees can cost more than an all-in 8%.

Can I manage my own rental property in Texas?

Yes. Texas owners can manage their own rental property without a real estate license. You are still responsible for following the Texas Property Code, including security deposit refunds within 30 days, flood disclosure, repair duties, rekeying between residents and fair housing law.

What do Texas landlords have to disclose about flooding?

Since January 1, 2022, Texas landlords must give residents a separate written notice at or before lease signing stating whether they know the home is in a 100-year floodplain and whether they know of flood damage to the home in the past five years (Tex. Prop. Code §92.0135).

When is hiring a property manager worth it?

It is usually worth it when you live out of town, own more than one rental, have had long vacancies or a bad placement, or do not have time for showings and repair calls. One extra month of vacancy can cost as much as a full year of management fees.
